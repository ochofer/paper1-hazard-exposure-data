This page explains how to run the data-building notebook from scratch, what each step should produce, and what to do when it produces something else. It also states plainly which parts of the analysis you can reproduce for nothing and which part you cannot.

If you are here to read rather than to run, the notebook itself is the better document. This page is the operational companion: the practical detail that would clutter the notebook but that you need if you are actually going to execute it.

The short version. Roughly two thirds of this project runs on free public data and needs about twenty minutes. The share price panel requires a paid subscription of about $150 for one month, and cannot legally be redistributed, so it is not in this repository. Everything else, including both blocking tests and the entire company-matching pipeline, is free.

What this project is

An empirical asset pricing study asking whether a company's exposure to physical climate hazards, measured from the locations of the physical assets it owns, helps explain its stock returns after controlling for the usual risk factors.

The notebook builds and audits the data for that study. It does not test the hypothesis. Three datasets are involved:

2. Get an OpenFIGI key FREE KEY

Register at openfigi.com/api. It is free and takes about a minute.

The key is optional, and the difference it makes is large enough to be worth the minute. Without one the service allows 25 requests per minute at 10 identifiers each. With one it allows 25 requests every 6 seconds at 100 each. On this dataset that is the difference between roughly a minute and most of a day.

The advertised monthly prices are usually the annually-billed rates. Paying monthly costs more per month and avoids a twelve-month commitment, which is the right trade for a one-off data pull. I paid about $150 for a single month, downloaded everything, archived it, and cancelled.

If you would rather not pay, sections 0, 1, 2 and 6 still run, and section 6 will run against any price panel you can supply in the same shape.

4. Store the keys in Colab Secrets, never in a cell READ

Colab has a Secrets panel, reached by the key icon in the left sidebar. Add FMP_API_KEY and OPENFIGI_API_KEY there, and switch on notebook access for each.

Do not paste a key into a cell. Notebook outputs are saved into the .ipynb file, so a key printed or pasted once is committed to version control and published along with everything else. This is one of the most common ways credentials leak from research repositories.

Running it

5. Open the notebook and run everything FREE

Use the Colab badge in the repository README, or this link:

https://colab.research.google.com/github/ochofer/paper1-hazard-exposure-data/blob/main/notebooks/01_raw_panels.ipynb

Then Runtime, Run all. Section 0 clones the repository onto the Colab machine by itself; there is nothing to configure.

Two copies exist and only one comes from that link. The link fetches the current notebook from GitHub. The configuration files it reads come from a second copy that the notebook clones into /content on the Colab machine. Section 0 synchronises that clone and prints the commit before and after, so a stale copy is visible rather than silent.

If the printed commit is older than the repository head, run !rm -rf /content/paper1-hazard-exposure-data in a cell and run again. Colab also caches notebooks, so open the link in a fresh tab rather than reloading an existing one.

What each section should produce

7. Section 1, the risk factors FREE

Expect roughly 4,000 daily rows for 2010 to 2025, which is about 252 trading days a year.

The quickest sanity check is the summary table. The minimum and maximum of the market factor should fall in March 2020, and they should be around -12 and +9 percent. Those are the two extreme days of the COVID crash and recovery. If they fall somewhere else, the dates were parsed wrongly.

If the parser raises "No YYYYMMDD rows found"

The URL is pointing at a monthly file rather than a daily one. Monthly files use six-digit dates and additionally append a block of annual data below the monthly data, which needs different handling. The notebook raises rather than returning an empty table precisely so this cannot pass unnoticed.

8. Section 2, building the company list FREE KEY

This is the longest section and the one where most of the judgement lives. It turns 328 ownership records into a list of tickers.

Expect the resolution to run in waves, most companies resolving in the first, and a final uncapped pass over a handful of large bond issuers. Results are cached to disk, so a second run costs almost nothing.

StepWatch for
2, resolutionRoughly 270 of 328 companies resolving to listed shares. A well-known listed company in the unresolved list is a matching failure, not evidence that it is unlisted
2b, one listing per companyThe line no symbol is claimed by two entities. If two companies share a ticker, the crosswalk has merged them
2c, currencyCurrency filled for every company, and the spot-check table showing European companies on European exchanges rather than US over-the-counter lines
2d, name retry and correctionsEach recovered name printed with its similarity score. Read them; a low score on an unfamiliar name is worth checking by hand

The spot-check table in 2c is the most valuable output in this section. A wrong listing does not produce an error or a missing value. It produces a plausible price series for the wrong security, and the only way to catch it is to look at the chosen tickers and recognise that something is off.

9. Sections 3 and 4, prices and checks PAID

The download takes a few minutes. Then section 4 runs sixteen checks and prints a table.

Read the whole table rather than the pass count. The checks are ordered by what they protect, not by importance, and a single failure can invalidate everything downstream while fifteen passes look reassuring.

Common failures and what they mean

More than one return convention. Some companies came back on total return and others on price return. The difference is a dividend yield, which in a utility-heavy sample is larger than most published effects. Find out which companies fell back and why before proceeding.

Duplicate company-date pairs. A paginated response returned overlapping pages. Any later regression would count those observations twice.

Fewer than 2,000 shared trading days. The factor and price calendars disagree. A later merge would silently discard most of the data.

A company returned no data. Expected on a restricted plan. Record which ones rather than quietly continuing with a smaller sample.

10. Sections 5 and 6, the blocking tests PAID FREE

These are the tests that decide whether the data can answer the question. Section 5 needs the subscription because it prices companies that no longer exist. Section 6 needs only the factor data and a price panel.

The thresholds for section 5 are written into the notebook above the cell that computes the number, which is the only point at which a threshold means anything. Section 6 checks that a diversified portfolio has a market beta near one, which is close to a definition rather than a hypothesis, and that regulated utilities come back less market-sensitive than integrated oil companies.

Expect section 6 to produce a positive alpha, and expect the notebook to argue with it. Sections 6b and 6c work through the mechanical explanations one at a time, ruling each out by measurement rather than by assertion. Following that argument is more instructive than the final answer.

11. Section 7, archiving FREE

Only relevant if you paid for price data. It copies everything to Google Drive, builds a single archive file, and verifies the copies by checksum.

Verify by checksum, not by file count. A truncated copy has the wrong size and would be caught by almost any check. A corrupted one has the right size and would not. The archive ships with a SHA256SUMS.txt inside it, so a copy can be verified at any point in future with:

shasum -a 256 -c SHA256SUMS.txt

Google Drive mounting from Colab fails intermittently with a credential error. The browser download is an equally good route, and keeping two copies in different places is the actual goal.

What cannot be reproduced, and why

12. The price panel is not in this repository READ

Financial Modeling Prep's terms require a separate licensing agreement to redistribute their data. So data/raw/ is excluded from version control and the price panel is not published here.

What is published instead:

  • The code that fetches it, so the download is inspectable.
  • The resolved ticker list in config/, which is my own work and is the part that took the effort.
  • The hand corrections in config/ticker_overrides.csv, each with a written reason.
  • A manifest recording row counts, date ranges and a SHA-256 for every file, so anyone who buys their own access can prove their panel matches mine rather than assuming it.

Panel A, the factor data, is freely redistributable and its exact downloaded bytes are archived, because its publisher revises the file in place and a download next year returns different content for the same nominal series.

Known limitations

13. What I would want a referee to know READ
LimitationStatus
The hazard exposure variable does not exist yet. The ownership dataset carries no asset coordinates, so building it needs the separate sector datasets plus a hazard datasetNot started. Needs no subscription
The ownership snapshot is undated. Using a current ownership map to explain past returns is look-ahead bias in the explanatory variable, which no downstream care repairsInvestigated. The publisher's earlier releases cannot reconstruct a historical map, because most change between releases is their own research catching up
Survivorship bias is unmeasured at the relevant company size. The price provider's delisting records do not reach back far enoughBounded as far as the source allows in section 5. CRSP would settle it
No currency conversion. Nine currencies, and UK prices are in pence rather than poundsDeliberately left to a separate, testable step
Most return variation sits in the last third of the sample, and both driving episodes correlate with hazard exposure by constructionMeasured in section 6c. Pre-committed to reporting split samples as standard rather than as robustness
Some asset owners are subsidiaries of listed parents. Whether to roll them up changes what counts as a companyOpen. A research design decision, not a lookup

If a null result emerges, I intend to report it as one alongside the minimum effect size the sample could have detected. A study that can only publish one of its two possible answers is not a test of anything.
