# Findings: data layer and blocking tests

**21 August 2026.** I measured everything below rather than assuming it. Every number
here is reproducible from `notebooks/01_raw_panels.ipynb`, and the raw outputs sit in
`data/raw/blocking_test_*.csv`, which I cannot commit under the price provider's
redistribution terms.

I wrote this as the evidence base for my methods and limitations sections, so it states
what I found and, just as often, what I could not measure.

### Crosswalk faults found, and why they matter

Each of these would have been invisible downstream, since none of them produces a
missing value. I found them by looking at the chosen tickers rather than at any summary
statistic.

- **Every listing line per firm was being downloaded.** Allianz appeared three times as
  `ALV`, `ALIZY` and `ALIZF`. A portfolio holding all three holds one bet with three
  weights, and near perfectly correlated duplicates inflate cross-sectional precision.
- **Preferred shares entered through name matching.** `UEPEP` stood in for Union Electric
  and its 42 assets; preferred is a bond-like claim and does not carry the equity return.
- **Berkshire resolved to `BRK-A`**, which trades a few hundred shares a day at roughly
  1,500 times the B-share price, giving stale prices and coarse discreteness.
- **Five European firms sat on thin US receipts.** Stora Enso was on a line trading
  $302k a day against $19.4m in Helsinki; also Fortum, Outokumpu, Neste and Henkel. A
  receipt carries an exchange rate move and a stale US close against a European session,
  so it is the wrong series rather than a noisy one.
- **The `lei` column held the literal string `"not found"` for 13 firms**, Chevron among
  them. As a string it is a valid join key, so all 13 were merged into one another.

All corrections are in `config/ticker_overrides.csv` with written reasons.

## 3. Blocking test 1: survivorship

**I could not answer this question with the price provider's data, and the reason is
decisive.**

Recorded delistings by year: 7 in 2010, 3 in 2012, 10 in 2015, then 1,843 in 2023 and
2,353 in 2025. A 28-fold rise. Delisting rates do not move like that; coverage does. The
list is effectively empty before 2016 and only dense from 2021, so **any rate computed
across the full window from this source is an artefact, not a lower bound.**

What I can say:

| Measure | Value | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Size and venue matched rate, 2021-2025 | 9.6 percent | Numerator matched on dollar volume, denominator on market cap. Not the same filter |
| Annualised | 1.93 percent | |
| Extrapolated to 16 years | 30.9 percent | An assumption, not a measurement |

**My more useful finding reverses the standard story.** Median final-year return against
the market, split by the firm's own final-year value traded:

| Final-year dollar volume | n | Median gap | Share negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| under $100k | 32 | -14.6% | 59% |
| $100k to $1m | 16 | -67.9% | 88% |
| $1m to $10m | 13 | +0.7% | 46% |
| **over $10m** | **10** | **+18.1%** | **30%** |

Small firms delist because they fail. Firms of the size in this universe mostly delist
because they are **acquired**, and acquisitions pay a premium. So excluding delisted firms
may bias returns **downward** here rather than upward. With ten observations I treat this as a direction rather
than a magnitude, and the honest statement is that **the bias is unmeasured at the sizes
that matter**.

The right instrument is CRSP's delisting file, complete from 1926 with delisting returns
attached. I have applied for access and do not yet have it. My free fallbacks are SEC
Form 25 filings on EDGAR for the US names, and the GLEIF `entity_status` field, which I
already hold.

## 4. Blocking test 2: factor replication

**Passed.** Market beta 0.917 equal-weighted and 1.03 value-weighted against the Fama and
French (1993) factors, R-squared 0.85, 4,023 days, median 134 firms a day. Standard errors
are Newey and West (1987) at five lags, fixed before I saw any result.

Individual betas against published expectations:

| Firm | Beta | Expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Duke Energy | 0.43 | utility, well below 1 |
| Southern Co | 0.44 | utility, well below 1 |
| American Electric Power | 0.45 | utility, well below 1 |
| NextEra | 0.60 | utility, below 1 |
| Exxon Mobil | 0.81 | energy, near 1 |
| Chevron | 0.94 | energy, near 1 |

The ordering is correct and the magnitudes match published estimates, so I am satisfied
that **the crosswalk is sound and the returns are real returns.**

### The alpha, and what it is not

The portfolio carries an alpha of 3.5 to 4.2 percent a year, and it is significant. I
eliminated two explanations by measurement rather than by argument.

**Not microstructure.** Across equal versus value weighting and daily versus monthly
buy-and-hold, the alpha moved 0.19 percentage points: 3.67, 2.83, 3.93, 3.49. The
daily-rebalancing bias documented by Blume and Stambaugh (1983) is not the cause, despite
26 listings trading under a million dollars a day.

**Not missing factors.** Adding the profitability and investment factors of Fama and
French (2015) and the momentum factor of Carhart (1997) *raised* it from 3.49 to 4.18 at
a t of 2.52. The CMA loading is -0.225, so the richer model expects even less from these
capital-intensive firms.

### What it is: a period effect

| Subperiod | Alpha, percent a year | t |
|---|---|---|
| 2010-2019, pre-COVID | +1.87 | 0.99 |
| 2020-2022, COVID and energy shock | +10.60 | 2.80 |
| 2023-2025, data-centre demand | +8.31 | 1.86 |

**Over a normal decade this portfolio has no alpha**, so my pipeline is not manufacturing
a premium. Everything sits after 2020 and is dateable: the COVID collapse and recovery, the
2022 European gas crisis which handed windfalls to power and energy names, and data-centre
electricity demand repricing the independent power producers.

Subperiods carry 36 observations against 6 factors, so read the alphas as indicative
magnitudes and the t-statistics as weak evidence.

### Why this is a design finding, not a plumbing one

Two thirds of my return variation sits in the last third of the window, so **any
cross-sectional sort I run will be dominated by it**. Both episodes correlate with the sort
variable by construction: the 2022 crisis was a shock to European energy assets, and the
data-centre trade is concentrated in US independent power producers, of which Vistra,
Constellation, Talen and NRG are all large owners in this universe.

A hazard sort could load on either and report it as a climate risk premium, with a
convincing t-statistic. I would rather know that before running the sort.

## 5. Pre-commitments, made before any hazard variable exists

I record these here so I cannot motivate them with a result later.

1. **Report the full sample as headline**, with pre-2020 and post-2020 splits alongside as
   standard rather than as robustness, and state that the sample contains one large
   energy-market regime change.
2. **Check the overlap** between high-hazard firms and the independent power producers
   before running the sort, and report the result with and without them.
3. **Survivorship is unmeasured at the relevant firm size.** Say so. Do not quote the 9.6
   percent figure as if it described this universe.
4. **Do not shorten the sample window** to fit data availability. A sample chosen by what a
   vendor will sell is a vendor-selected sample and belongs in the limitations section.
5. **A rigorous null result is success.** Report the minimum detectable effect so a null
   is informative. That number now exists: **6.57% a year at ten years**, against a
   plausible true effect near 2%. The cross-sectional test cannot detect the effect it was
   designed to detect, and I report that rather than working around it.

## 6. What is still missing

**The exposure variable does not exist yet.** The GEM Ownership Tracker carries no
coordinates: three of nine sheets have a `GEM location ID`, which is a key rather than a
place, and the mine, pipeline, steel and cement sheets have no location field at all.
To build physical hazard exposure I need the individual sector trackers, a hazard
dataset, the join and the firm-level aggregation. None of that needs the subscription.

**The ownership snapshot is undated and will stay that way.** I asked Global Energy
Monitor directly, and they confirmed they do not record when an ownership change took
effect and that historical ownership is not available outside their internal database.
They sent me sixteen files covering fourteen monthly releases, March 2025 to August
2026, so I could try to build a time series from the differences between them.

That does not work, and I measured why. Over eighteen months, the direct owner of an
asset changed on 5.2% of assets, while the top-level parent I actually attribute to
changed on 46.4%. Both numbers describe the same eighteen months of real corporate
activity, so the gap is not the world changing. It is the ownership graph growing as the
database is researched. Decomposing the apparent change: 64.0% build-out, 20.0%
restatement, 16.0% methodology change.

So I treat ownership as fixed as at the August 2026 release, state the measurement date,
and bound the resulting error using the 5.2% figure. This is a property of the source
rather than a shortcut, and I report it that way.

**I have not done the currency conversion.** Nine currencies, and GBp is pence rather
than pounds.

**Two subsidiary roll-up decisions are still open**, Union Electric and Entergy
Louisiana, carrying 69 assets between them. Whether to fold a wholly owned subsidiary
into its listed parent changes what counts as a company in my sample, so it is a design
decision rather than a lookup and I have not made it silently.
